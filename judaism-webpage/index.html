Judaism: Faith, History, and Tradition

Judaism is one of the world’s oldest living religions. This site explores what Jews believe, how they pray and celebrate, the places they hold sacred, and where Judaism and Christianity share common ground — and where they differ.

Part 1

What Is Judaism?

Judaism is the religion, culture, and way of life of the Jewish people. It began more than 3,000 years ago in the ancient Near East with Abraham, whom Jews see as the first person to enter a covenant (a sacred agreement) with God. Judaism is monotheistic: it teaches that there is only one God. Three big ideas sit at its center — God, Torah, and Israel.

God

Jews believe in one God who created the universe, is all-powerful, and cares about how people live. God has no body and cannot be divided into parts. This belief is summed up in the Shema, prayed every morning and evening: “Hear, O Israel! The Lord is our God, the Lord alone!” (Deuteronomy 6:4, NABRE). Out of respect, many Jews do not say God’s personal name aloud and instead use titles like Adonai (“my Lord”) or HaShem (“the Name”).

Torah

Torah means “teaching” or “instruction.” In the narrow sense, it is the Five Books of Moses: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy. Jewish tradition teaches that God revealed the Torah to Moses at Mount Sinai. It contains Israel’s early history and the 613 commandments (mitzvot) that guide Jewish life — from how to pray and eat to how to treat neighbors and strangers. In a wider sense, “Torah” can mean all Jewish teaching.

Israel

“Israel” has three connected meanings. First, it is the people: the name God gave to Jacob (Genesis 32:29), whose twelve sons became the twelve tribes, so Jews are called the “Children of Israel.” Second, it is the land God promised to Abraham and his descendants. Third, it is the modern State of Israel, founded in 1948. Being part of Israel means sharing in the covenant — a relationship with God and with other Jews.

The Three Sources of Sacred Jewish Writings

תנ״ך

Tanakh — the Hebrew Bible

The Tanakh is the Jewish Bible, made up of 24 books. Its name is an acronym of its three parts (T‑N‑K):

  • Torah (Teaching) — the Five Books of Moses, e.g. Genesis and Exodus
  • Nevi’im (Prophets) — e.g. Joshua, Isaiah, Jeremiah
  • Ketuvim (Writings) — e.g. Psalms, Proverbs, Esther

A section of the Torah is read in synagogue every week, so the whole Torah is completed each year.

תלמוד

Talmud — the Oral Torah written down

Jews believe Moses also received an Oral Torah that explains how to live out the written commandments. To preserve it, rabbis wrote it down. The Talmud has two layers:

  • Mishnah — organized laws, edited around 200 CE by Rabbi Judah the Prince
  • Gemara — centuries of rabbinic discussion and debate about the Mishnah

The Babylonian Talmud (completed around 500 CE) is the most studied. For example, the tractate Berakhot covers blessings and daily prayer, and the tractate Shabbat explains how to keep the Sabbath.

מדרש

Midrash — interpreting the text

Midrash comes from a Hebrew word meaning “to search out.” It is the rabbis’ way of reading between the lines of the Bible to find deeper meaning. There are two main kinds:

  • Midrash Halakhah — explains laws, e.g. Mekhilta on the Book of Exodus
  • Midrash Aggadah — stories and lessons, e.g. Genesis Rabbah

A famous example from Genesis Rabbah tells how young Abraham smashed the idols in his father’s shop — a story not found in Genesis itself, but used to teach his faith in one God.

Weekly holy day

The Importance of Shabbat

Shabbat (the Sabbath) is the most important holy day in Judaism — and it comes every week. It begins at sunset on Friday and ends after nightfall on Saturday. Shabbat remembers that God rested on the seventh day of creation (Genesis 2:2–3), and keeping it holy is one of the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:8–11).

On Shabbat, observant Jews stop ordinary work — the Mishnah lists 39 categories of forbidden work, such as cooking, building, and writing. Instead, the day is set aside for rest, prayer, family, and study.

  • Friday evening: candles are lit before sunset, and a blessing over wine (Kiddush) and braided bread (challah) begins the festive meal.
  • Saturday: families attend synagogue, where the weekly Torah portion is read.
  • Saturday night: the Havdalah ceremony, with a braided candle, wine, and sweet spices, separates Shabbat from the new week.

Shabbat has helped hold the Jewish people together for thousands of years. No matter where Jews lived or how they were treated, they could still stop every week to rest, pray, and be together as a family.

One religion, many ways of practicing

Not all Jews practice the same way. The main movements in the United States are Orthodox (keeps traditional Jewish law closely), Conservative (keeps Jewish law but allows some change over time), Reform (sees Jewish law as a guide that each person can interpret), and Reconstructionist. When this site says “Jews believe,” it describes mainstream Jewish teaching — individual beliefs and practices vary.

Part 2

Jewish Signs and Symbols

Many Jewish symbols come straight from commandments in the Torah. Others grew out of history and tradition. Each one is a reminder of God, the covenant, or the story of the Jewish people.

Chai חי

Chai means “alive” or “living.” It is spelled with the Hebrew letters chet and yud, and it is often worn on necklaces. It celebrates the value of life, echoing the toast L’chaim — “To life!” Because Hebrew letters also stand for numbers (chet = 8, yud = 10), chai equals 18, so Jews often give gifts or donations in multiples of 18.

Kippah כיפה

A kippah (called a yarmulke in Yiddish) is a small, round cap. Wearing it is a sign of respect and a reminder that God is always above us. It is a custom rather than a written law in the Torah. Many Orthodox men wear one all day; in Conservative and Reform communities, men and often women wear one during prayer and study.

Dreidel סביבון

A dreidel is a four-sided spinning top used in a game during Hanukkah. Each side has a Hebrew letter — nun, gimel, hei, shin — standing for “Nes gadol hayah sham,” “A great miracle happened there.” In Israel, the last letter is pei, for “here.” The game is a fun way to retell the Hanukkah story.

Menorah מנורה

The menorah is a seven-branched lampstand. God commanded Moses to make one of pure gold for the Tabernacle (Exodus 25:31–40), and it later stood in the Temple in Jerusalem. The carving shown here, on Rome’s Arch of Titus, shows Roman soldiers carrying it away after the Temple was destroyed in 70 CE. Today it is the official emblem of the State of Israel. (The nine-branched lamp used at Hanukkah is a different object, called a hanukkiah.)

Mezuzah מזוזה

A mezuzah is a small case attached to the right-hand doorpost of a Jewish home. Inside is a handwritten parchment with the Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4–9 and 11:13–21), which commands, “write them on the doorposts of your houses” (Deuteronomy 6:9, NABRE). Many Jews touch or kiss the mezuzah when they walk through the door, as a reminder that God is present in their home.

Shofar שופר

The shofar is a ram’s horn blown like a trumpet. It is sounded on Rosh Hashanah and at the very end of Yom Kippur, and during the month before the High Holy Days. Its piercing blasts are a wake-up call to repent and return to God. The ram’s horn also recalls the ram that Abraham offered in place of his son Isaac (Genesis 22).

Star of David מגן דוד

In Hebrew it is the Magen David, the “Shield of David,” named after King David. It is made of two overlapping triangles. The shape is ancient, but it became the most widely recognized symbol of the Jewish people only in the last few centuries. It appears on synagogues, on jewelry, and in the center of Israel’s flag.

Tallit & Tzitzit טלית וציצית

A tallit is a prayer shawl worn during morning prayers. On its four corners are tzitzit — specially knotted fringes. The Torah commands Israel to wear them so that whenever they see the fringes, they will remember all of God’s commandments and keep them (Numbers 15:37–41). Some Jews also wear a small fringed garment called a tallit katan under their clothes every day.

Tefillin תפילין

Tefillin are two small black leather boxes with straps. Inside are parchments with four passages from the Torah, including the Shema. During weekday morning prayers, one box is bound on the arm (near the heart) and the other on the forehead (near the mind). They fulfill the command to bind God’s words “on your arm as a sign and … as a pendant on your forehead” (Deuteronomy 6:8, NABRE).

Torah Scroll & Yad ספר תורה ויד

A Torah scroll is the holiest object in a synagogue. A trained scribe (sofer) writes all five books by hand on parchment, a job that can take about a year. The scroll is kept in the Ark and read aloud on Shabbat, Mondays, Thursdays, and holidays. Readers follow the words with a yad (“hand”) — a pointer shaped like a hand with a pointing finger — so the parchment is not touched or damaged.

Jewish Festivals

The Jewish calendar is lunisolar: months follow the moon, but an extra month is added seven times every 19 years so holidays stay in the right season. That is why Jewish holidays fall on different dates on the regular calendar each year. Every Jewish day also begins at sunset, so holidays start in the evening.

1–2 Tishrei · September or October

Rosh Hashanah ראש השנה

Rosh Hashanah (“head of the year”) is the Jewish New Year and the start of the High Holy Days. It celebrates the creation of the world and is a day when God judges all people. The shofar is blown in synagogue, and families eat apples dipped in honey to wish for a sweet new year. It opens the Ten Days of Repentance, a time to ask forgiveness from God and from other people.

10 Tishrei · September or October

Yom Kippur יום כיפור

Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, is the holiest day of the Jewish year. Adults fast for about 25 hours — no food or water — and spend much of the day in synagogue confessing sins and asking God for forgiveness. Many people wear white as a sign of purity. The day ends with the closing prayer, Neilah, and one long blast of the shofar.

15–21 Tishrei · September or October

Sukkot סוכות

Sukkot, the Festival of Booths, lasts seven days. Families build a sukkah, a temporary hut with a roof of branches, and eat meals in it. This recalls the 40 years the Israelites lived in temporary shelters in the desert after leaving Egypt (Leviticus 23:42–43). It is also a harvest festival: people wave the lulav (palm, myrtle, and willow branches) with an etrog (a citrus fruit).

Begins 25 Kislev · November or December

Hanukkah חנוכה

Hanukkah (“dedication”) is an eight-day festival of lights. Around 164 BCE, a small Jewish group called the Maccabees defeated the Greek-Syrian army of King Antiochus IV and rededicated the Temple in Jerusalem. According to the Talmud, a one-day supply of oil kept the Temple menorah burning for eight days. Jews light one more candle on the hanukkiah each night, play dreidel, and eat foods fried in oil, like latkes and doughnuts.

15–22 Nisan · March or April

Pesach (Passover) פסח

Pesach celebrates God freeing the Israelites from slavery in Egypt, as told in the Book of Exodus. The name comes from the story of God “passing over” the Israelites’ homes during the tenth plague. It lasts seven days in Israel and eight outside it. Families hold a seder, a special meal where they read the Haggadah, ask the Four Questions, and eat symbolic foods like matzah (unleavened bread) and bitter herbs. No leavened bread is eaten all week.

27 Nisan · April or May

Yom HaShoah יום השואה

Yom HaShoah is Holocaust Remembrance Day. It honors the six million Jews murdered by Nazi Germany and its collaborators, and the Jews who resisted. Israel’s parliament set the date in 1951, close to the anniversary of the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising. It is a modern day of mourning, not a celebration: in Israel a siren sounds and the whole country stands still in silence, and Jewish communities around the world light memorial candles and read survivors’ names and stories.

Part 4

Sacred Places

Jews can pray anywhere, but a few places hold a special place in Jewish hearts. The two most important are the city of Jerusalem and the Western Wall inside it.

Geographical area

Jerusalem ירושלים

About 3,000 years ago, King David made Jerusalem his capital, and his son Solomon built the First Temple there — the one place where sacrifices to God were offered. The Babylonians destroyed it in 586 BCE. The Second Temple was later built on the same site and destroyed by the Romans in 70 CE.

Even after being scattered around the world, Jews never stopped longing for Jerusalem:

  • Synagogues are built so that people pray facing Jerusalem.
  • Daily prayers ask God to rebuild Jerusalem.
  • The Passover seder and Yom Kippur end with the words “Next year in Jerusalem!”
  • At Jewish weddings, a glass is broken, in part to remember the destroyed Temple.
Specific place

The Western Wall הכותל

The Western Wall, or Kotel, is part of the retaining wall that King Herod built around the Temple Mount in the first century BCE. When the Romans destroyed the Second Temple, this wall survived. It is the closest place to the site of the ancient Temple where Jews are able to pray today, which makes it the holiest place of Jewish prayer.

  • People pray there day and night; men and women pray in separate sections.
  • Visitors slip handwritten prayers into the cracks between the stones.
  • Many families celebrate bar and bat mitzvahs there.
  • Crowds gather for holidays and to mourn the Temple on the fast of Tisha B’Av.

For Jews worldwide, the Wall is a living link to the Temple and a sign that the Jewish people have survived.

Judaism in South Florida

South Florida has one of the largest Jewish communities in the United States. Here are two historic synagogues in Miami Beach — both well south of West Palm Beach. A synagogue is a Jewish house of prayer, study, and community.

Temple Emanu-El

Address
1701 Washington Avenue, Miami Beach, FL 33139
Website
tesobe.org
Movement
Conservative

Temple Emanu-El’s building was completed in 1948. With its huge copper dome and Byzantine and Moorish style, it is one of the best-known landmarks in South Beach.

Services offered

  • Shabbat evening services on Friday, followed by an Oneg (social gathering)
  • Shabbat morning services on Saturday, followed by Kiddush
  • Weekday morning minyan (group prayer) and a Sunday morning minyan with breakfast
  • High Holy Day and holiday services
  • Bar and bat mitzvahs, weddings, and other life-cycle celebrations
  • Public tours of the historic sanctuary

Temple Menorah

Address
620 75th Street, Miami Beach, FL 33141
Website
templemenorahmiami.org
Movement
Conservative

Founded in 1949 in North Beach, Temple Menorah became a spiritual home for Holocaust survivors, Jewish refugees from Cuba, and Jews who left the Soviet Union. Its building was remodeled by the famous Miami Beach architect Morris Lapidus.

Services offered

  • Carlebach-style Kabbalat Shabbat (musical service welcoming Shabbat) on Friday evenings
  • Saturday morning Shabbat services with Torah reading and an interactive Torah study led by the rabbi
  • Daily minyan twice a day, morning and evening
  • A monthly family service so children can take part at their own pace, plus weekly babysitting
  • Youth, adult, family, and senior programs

Service times change during the year, so check each synagogue’s website for the current schedule.

Part 6

Similarities and Differences with Christianity

Christianity began within Judaism — Jesus, his mother Mary, and the apostles were all Jewish. Because of this, the two religions share a great deal. But they are two separate faiths, and they understand some key beliefs very differently.

= Similarities

1. Belief in One God

Both Judaism and Christianity are monotheistic: they worship the one God who created the world, made a covenant with Abraham, and spoke through Moses and the prophets. The Jewish prayer called the Shema declares, Hear, O Israel! The Lord is our God, the Lord alone! (Deuteronomy 6:4, NABRE). Jesus himself quoted this same prayer when asked which commandment was the greatest: The first is this: ‘Hear, O Israel! The Lord our God is Lord alone!’ (Mark 12:29, NABRE). For both religions, loving this one God with your whole heart is the most important commandment.

2. Shared Sacred Scriptures

The books of the Jewish Tanakh are also part of the Christian Bible, where they make up most of the Old Testament (the Catholic Old Testament also includes a few extra books, like Tobit and 1–2 Maccabees, and the books are in a different order). Both religions read these texts in worship, pray the Psalms, and learn from the stories of Abraham, Moses, and the prophets. The Catholic Church teaches that it cannot forget that she received the revelation of the Old Testament through the people with whom God in His inexpressible mercy concluded the Ancient Covenant (Nostra Aetate, no. 4). The same document adds that the spiritual patrimony common to Christians and Jews is thus so great that the two should grow in mutual understanding and respect (Nostra Aetate, no. 4).

≠ Differences

1. The Messiah

Both religions speak of a Messiah (“anointed one”), but they disagree about who he is. Christians believe Jesus of Nazareth is the Messiah (the Christ) and the Son of God, who died, rose from the dead, and will come again. Judaism teaches that the Messiah has not yet come. In traditional Jewish belief, the Messiah will be a human descendant of King David who will bring peace to the world, gather the Jewish people to Israel, and rebuild the Temple; since this has not happened, Jews do not accept Jesus as the Messiah. (Many Reform Jews speak of a future messianic age of peace rather than one person.) The Catechism describes both sides as waiting, but for different things: one awaits the return of the Messiah who died and rose from the dead and is recognized as Lord and Son of God; the other awaits the coming of a Messiah, whose features remain hidden till the end of time (Catechism of the Catholic Church, no. 840).

2. The Notion of God: One God, or One God in Three Persons?

Although both religions worship one God, they describe God differently. Christians believe in the Trinity: one God who exists as three divine Persons — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — and that God the Son became human in Jesus. As the Church teaches, We do not confess three Gods, but one God in three persons (Catechism of the Catholic Church, no. 253). Judaism teaches that God is absolutely one and cannot be divided, and that God does not take on a human body. For Jews, the Shema’s words that the Lord is our God, the Lord alone (Deuteronomy 6:4, NABRE), mean that no human being, including Jesus, is divine.

3. The Sabbath and Sunday

Jews keep the Sabbath on the seventh day, from Friday sunset to Saturday night, following the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:8–11). Most Christians, including Catholics, gather for worship on Sunday instead, because Jesus rose from the dead on the first day of the week. For Catholics, Sunday is the Lord’s Day, celebrated with Mass. The Catechism explains: Sunday is expressly distinguished from the sabbath which it follows chronologically every week; for Christians its ceremonial observance replaces that of the sabbath (Catechism of the Catholic Church, no. 2175). So while both religions set aside one holy day each week for rest and worship, they keep different days for different reasons.

Study help

Key Terms

Covenant

A sacred agreement between God and the people of Israel, first made with Abraham and renewed with Moses at Mount Sinai.

Mitzvah (plural: mitzvot)

A commandment from God. The Torah contains 613 of them. The word is also used for any good deed.

Rabbi

“My teacher.” A person trained in Jewish law and tradition who teaches, leads prayer, and guides a community.

Synagogue

A Jewish house of prayer, study, and gathering. The Torah scrolls are kept there in the Ark.

Kashrut (kosher)

The Jewish dietary laws. For example, they forbid pork and shellfish and require meat and dairy to be kept separate.

Bar / Bat Mitzvah

“Son / daughter of the commandment.” At 13 for boys (12 or 13 for girls), a young Jew becomes responsible for keeping the commandments, often marked by reading from the Torah.

Diaspora

Jewish communities living outside the Land of Israel.

Shoah

The Hebrew word for the Holocaust, meaning “catastrophe.”
